FSR Series: Autonomous Field-Scouting Robots for Precision Agriculture

SolidWorks FEA ROS 2 Nav2 PCL LiDAR-Inertial SLAM (LIO-SAM) CAN Bus
7.5 h18.7 km at 2.6 km/h average
30°incline climb with a 20 kg payload
18 Waverage drivetrain power
400 HzCAN update rate for real-time slip detection

Six-wheel autonomous scouting robot for orchard and vineyard rows, with suspension, traction, power and navigation solved as one system.

My role: I designed and developed the full stack: chassis, custom airless-tire FEA, drivetrain and wiring architecture, and navigation/sensing software.

Mechanical Platform

Canopy-stable chassis: keeps the sensor deck steady on sloped, rutted rows, so most terrain disturbance stays at the wheel, not the deck.

  • Three independently articulated axles (front, center, back), each rolling about its own pivot, with the center chassis also pitching relative to the front and back pairs.
  • Measured isolation: 20° front-axle roll → 9° body roll; 5° middle-axle roll → 4° body roll; 16° back-axle roll → 6.5° body roll.

Figure 1: Canopy-stable chassis.

Custom airless tire:

A lugged, TPU-printed tire with an internal spoke structure that removes punctures in thorny rows and houses the in-wheel motor. Two spoke patterns were compared in SolidWorks FEA; load cases for the selected production design:

Figure 2: Airless tire, designed and analyzed in SolidWorks FEA.

Drive and Control

CAN bus: 400 Hz per-motor slip detection. Catching wheel slip means reading motor current fast enough to tie a current drop to loss of ground contact, on a bus that also carries real-time control commands and status polling for all six motors. The first implementation bottlenecked on three causes:

  • Blocking timeouts: one slow motor stalled the entire round-robin polling cycle.
  • Frame-type parsing: frame types 0x02 and 0x12 extract motor IDs differently, so early parser logic misattributed telemetry.
  • TX collisions: near-simultaneous replies dropped frames.

The fix was a non-blocking request carousel that sorts responses into a unified 6-motor state array regardless of arrival order. Disambiguating the frame types and refining bus arbitration resolved the collisions, pushing throughput to 400 Hz per motor.

Autonomy

LiDAR-inertial SLAM in sparse open fields. Flat ground and low, repetitive canopy cause severe geometric degeneracy: in FAST-LIO2, aggressive high-frequency frame updates kept matching against ambiguous geometry, producing noise, rapid drift, and localization failure. Default SLAM architectures also assumed idealized flat motion, giving an incorrect transform tree for a rover on unstabilized, uneven terrain where pitch and roll distort frame relationships. LIO-SAM, adapted to sparse agricultural rows, was the solution:

Figure 4: Autonomous driving on uneven terrain.

Terrain-aware traversability.

Ruts, banks, and drop-offs are a continuous property of the ground, not discrete objects a LiDAR return flags, so a standard obstacle layer can't represent them. A ROS 2 node runs PCL surface-normal estimation on the deskewed LIO-SAM point cloud and classifies terrain in real time as traversable, steep, vertical, or cliff, publishing each class as its own cloud. Nav2 treats "traversable" as free space and everything else as an obstacle region.

Figure 5: Terrain-aware obstacle detection.

Automated silkworm-rearing line: a sealed rack of about 200 pods served by three coordinated robots, with no one ever entering the room.

My role: I owned the engineering end to end, designing and building the full line from mechanism and sensing through firmware, motion control, and multi-agent coordination. It has been in operation for 24 months.

One pod, four stages
  1. ~10 sConveyor to hatch
  2. ~4 sHatch docking
  3. ~3 sHand-off, shutter closes
  4. <20 sPlacement, horizontal and vertical at once

Full cycle: about 45 seconds. The legacy system took about 3 minutes.

Multi-Agent Coordination

Three agents handing off one pod in sequence are only fast if each stages itself ahead of the pod's arrival, not after being told to.

  • Peer-to-peer broadcasting: a Flask server handles human input and top-level job assignment; the conveyor, hatch, and carriage exchange intent directly over MQTT via their own Wi-Fi modules.
  • Predictive staging: agents broadcast near-term intent instead of waiting on a server command, so the hatch opens before a pod arrives and the carriage positions itself before docking finishes.
  • Sub-second hand-off: this peer coordination keeps dead time between adjacent stages under a second.
  • Throughput: a 4× speedup over the legacy 3-minute place-based system, driving the 3–5× overall gain.

Figure 1: Control flow : server, Wi-Fi/MQTT, execution agents, CAN-bus actuators.

Embedded Firmware Architecture

Each agent runs this coordination on one low-power microcontroller that also handles real-time motion control and sensing, so network latency can never jitter a motor command

  • Isolated tasks: each agent runs FreeRTOS with motion control, sensing, and networking as separate tasks instead of one shared loop, so a slow or blocking operation on one can never stall another. The motion-profile calculation gets its own task, kept off the CAN-bus command path so its heavier computation can never jitter actuator output.
  • Task synchronization: semaphores mediate all inter-task data exchange through a central governing task on the agent's MCU, so tasks can't corrupt shared data or stall one another.
  • Shared awareness without the server: each agent publishes its state to a dedicated MQTT topic and subscribes to its peers', giving shared state awareness without server arbitration; lockstep handoffs use an alternating publish/acknowledge handshake.
  • Heartbeat startup gating: a steady server heartbeat acts as a system-wide watchdog, so an agent that can't see it at startup reboots and reconnects rather than acting on stale information.

Precision Motion Profiling

Time is essential when serving a large number of pods, so the carriage is optimized to maximize delivery and retrieval speed. Its actuators run Sigmoid motion profiles with payload-scheduled acceleration and deceleration profiles, giving smooth motion throughout every task.

Figure 2: Motion Profiling for different payloads.

Miyama Project: Cleanroom Material-Handling & Control Suite

PythonFlaskREST APIMQTTSQLAlchemySQLiteJavaScriptSwitchBot API

Server software and browser interface that turn operator actions into validated, logged commands for the cleanroom robots.

My role: I developed the full software stack: server architecture, backend APIs, task protocol, state and database layer, sensor integration, and the operator interface.

Architecture

  • Frontend: one browser application (Dashboard, Manual Control, Scheduler, and Task History) with rack selection, instead of a separate tool per function.
  • Backend: Flask application exposing REST endpoints and coordinating system state.
  • Messaging: MQTT carries task commands to the carriage.
  • Persistence: SQLite through SQLAlchemy for pod state and operation records, JSON files for schedules, and date-organized image storage.
  • Sensors: SwitchBot API polled for environmental device status.

Figure 1: Miyama software architecture.

Command Pipeline: Validation and Task Protocol

Operator UI → REST endpoint → validation → task encoding → MQTT → carriage

  • Unified position IDs: the physical rack is modeled as a coordinate grid (side, column, row), and one ID scheme addresses every pod position identically across the UI, database, and hardware commands.
  • State-aware validation: each carriage command (Take Out, Put In, Go To, Pick/Drop at the Air Lock Hatch) is checked against current pod occupancy and the previous action, and invalid sequences are rejected before anything is published.
  • Compact task protocol: the server encodes each task (type, coordinates, timing) into a compact character-based command and decodes it back, keeping carriage communication predictable and lightweight.
  • HTTP-to-MQTT bridge: Flask converts each validated HTTP request into an MQTT command published to the carriage.
  • Replayable workflows: a drag-and-drop scheduler turns one-off commands into saved, reorderable, multi-step sequences stored as JSON and replayed on demand.

State and Traceability

  • Persistent pod state: SQLite through SQLAlchemy stores each position's current status and when it last changed, updating as operations occur so the rack view reflects current pod state.
  • Operation history: every action, whether manual, scheduled, or an image capture, writes a timestamped record (operation, rack side, column, row) and is exposed through a filterable history endpoint.
  • Image records: captured images are logged the same way and tied to the pod position and event that produced them, then stored in date-organized folders instead of as anonymous files.

Environmental Monitoring

  • Sensor coverage: a CO₂ meter and thermo-hygrometers inside and outside the cleanroom/building, reporting temperature, humidity, CO₂, and battery state.
  • Backend integration: the server polls the SwitchBot API periodically with authenticated request headers, maintains status for each device, and exposes current values to the frontend.

Delta-parallel robot with topology-optimized links that stay stiff along the drive axis but flex on impact, protecting delicate produce.

My role: I designed the mechanism as a part of my master's research, ran the topology optimization and FEA validation on the link geometry, built the physical prototype, and built a custom vision-based tracking system to physically test real-world deformability against the simulation.

Compliant Link Design

The core idea: give the link anisotropic stiffness instead of uniform rigidity — stiff enough along the drive axis to hold positioning accuracy, compliant enough orthogonally to deform on impact instead of transmitting force to the produce.

  • Topology-optimized links: link geometry optimized for directional (anisotropic) structural strength rather than uniform stiffness, transferring drive force efficiently while staying flexible on lateral impact.
  • 3D-printed ABS/PLA: semi-flexible plastics in place of machined metal links, cutting both weight and per-unit manufacturing cost.

Figure 1: Topology-optimized link geometry.

FEA Validation

Run per arm, not on the full assembly: simulate the link's displacement under load in the two orthogonal directions, then take the ratio of X-direction displacement to Y-direction displacement. A higher ratio means more give in the intended flex direction relative to the intended stiff direction — exactly the behavior needed to absorb a hit without damaging whatever the arm touches.

  • Method: FEA in CREO Parametric Simulate, comparing a solid-stem link against the topology-optimized specimen.
  • Result: X/Y displacement ratio of 1.148 for the solid link versus 2.074 for the optimized link, confirming the optimized geometry is meaningfully more anisotropic — stiffer where it needs to be, more compliant where it needs to be.

Figure 2: FEA displacement ratio, solid vs. optimized link.

Failure Mode & Collision Testing

Compliance introduces its own risk: a link that bends can also let the ball joint work loose. I quantified that risk before calling the design safe, then tested the full robot under an actual collision.

  • Failure mode analysis: modeled ball-joint dislocation from link deflection under collision; worst-case clearance came out to 39.52% of the ball-joint radius — confirmed negligible to end-effector precision.
  • Collision response: under lateral impact on the full robot, the compliant arm still reaches 68% of its target displacement beyond where a rigid link would've stopped — absorbing impact force through deformation instead of transmitting it.

Crawler platform with a compliant delta arm that reaches beneath foliage to photograph pathogens overhead scanning misses.

My role: Designed and developed an autonomous crawler platform integrated with a compliant delta-arm manipulator capable of reaching beneath vegetable foliage, implemented the YOLO-based detection pipeline and PID arm-control program, and integrated both with the ORB-SLAM and ROS/Ardupilot navigation stack.

System Architecture

The platform pairs a tracked crawler base (Ardupilot + ORB-SLAM3 pose estimation, 4hr / 290Wh continuous operation, 25kg payload) with the delta arm mounted overhead. Two programs run independently and exchange data over ROS: a detection pipeline that locates plants and tracks the nearest target, and an arm-control program that drives the manipulator toward it with PID control.

  • Detection pipeline: YOLO-based plant detection extracts target position, offset-from-center, and confidence per frame, packaged and published to ROS.
  • Arm control: consumes the detection packet, computes the closest target, and drives the delta arm's 3 base servos plus a 4th-axis camera tilt to view upward from beneath the canopy.

Figure 1: Platform architecture and detection/arm-control data flow.

Data-Collection Methods, Field-Compared

Rather than assume the delta arm was worth the added complexity, I designed three data-gathering strategies and tested all three against potted broccoli under real field conditions to quantify the tradeoff.

  • Passive (in-motion): continuous overhead imaging while driving — fastest (5s), but the resulting 3D reconstruction had no information beneath the leaves.
  • Active (stop-and-scan): arm circles each plant at multiple heights for a 360° view — 21× slower (105s), meaningfully better leaf-surface and edge detail, but still couldn't see under the canopy.
  • Under-foliage (delta arm): camera tilted upward and inserted beneath the leaves — the only method that successfully imaged pathogens on the underside of leaves and stalks.

Figure 2: The three data-gathering strategies tested.

Field Results & Failure Modes

I treated the field test as validation, not a demo — and documented where the design still fell short, since that's what drives the next revision.

  • Result: the under-foliage method was the only one of the three to successfully collect data from occluded regions where pathogens actually originate.
  • Failure mode — snagging: outward-reaching leaves occasionally caught in the arm's ball joints during continuous motion, causing minor tearing.
  • Failure mode — imaging: chromatic aberration appeared in upward-angled shots under direct sunlight, and photogrammetry coverage still failed on drooping/occluding leaves.
  • Design limit identified: arm compliance was insufficient for young, tender stems in continuous motion — flagged rigidity/payload threshold as the target for the next iteration.

Figure 3: Pathogens (red markers) imaged beneath the canopy — the only method to succeed here.
